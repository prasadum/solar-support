Before we start

What are we designing?

A stand-alone (off-grid) solar system works without KSEB supply. Sunlight charges a battery in the day; the battery runs our lights, fans and TV at any time.

Think of the system as a bank account. The battery is the account. The solar panel earns money (charging = deposit). The fan and lights spend it (load = withdrawal). If we spend more than we earn, the account becomes empty and the lights go off.

So we must first know how much we spend every day (the load energy). Only then can we decide how big the battery and the panel must be. That is why the design goes in the reverse direction.

Survey the equipment and note the wattage

Walk through the house. List every electrical item, how many there are, and the power (watts) of each.

Where do we find the wattage?

Every appliance has a small nameplate or sticker. Look for a number with W (watts). If it shows only volts and amps, multiply them: W ≈ V × A. For example, a fan showing 230 V, 0.35 A uses about 230 × 0.35 = 80 W.

Energy used per day (watt-hours)

Watts tells how fast energy is used. Watt-hours tells how much energy is used in a day. The battery and panel are sized from watt-hours.

Energy (Wh) = Total watts × Hours used per day

A 100 W bulb ON for 1 hour uses 100 Wh. ON for 5 hours, it uses 100 × 5 = 500 Wh. 1000 Wh = 1 kWh = 1 unit on the KSEB bill.

Why hours matterA 40 W tube light ON for 6 hours (240 Wh) uses more energy than a 100 W computer ON for 2 hours (200 Wh). A small load used for a long time can need a bigger battery than a large load used for a short time.
Which month should we use?Use the hours of the critical design month: the month when the load is highest and sunshine is lowest. In Kerala this is usually during the monsoon (June to August), when lights are ON for more hours and the sky is cloudy.
Ask the house owner honestlyAsk "from what time to what time is the fan ON?" instead of "how many hours?". People usually under-estimate. A wrong number here makes every later step wrong.
Quick check: 2 tube lights of 40 W, ON 6 hours. Energy?

2 × 40 = 80 W; 80 × 6 = 480 Wh

Inverter sizing: the factors we must apply

The inverter must supply all the loads together. We apply four factors and then choose the next standard size from the market.

Factor 1 · Power factor (PF)

Inverters are rated in VA (volt-ampere), not watts. Fans, tube-light chokes and motors take some extra current that does no useful work. So the inverter must give more VA than the watts.

Inverter VA = Load watts ÷ Power factor (PF ≈ 0.8 for home loads)

Factor 2 · Inverter efficiency (η)

An inverter loses some energy as heat. With 90 % efficiency, to give 90 W of AC it takes 100 W from the battery. So the battery must supply more energy than the load uses.

Inverter input energy = Load Wh ÷ η
Inverter input power = Load W ÷ η

Factor 3 · Safety margin / future load

The book example selects the next standard size above the calculated VA. In field practice, designers add 20–25 % extra so the inverter does not run at full load all the time, and a new fan can be added later.

Factor 4 · Starting surge

A motor load (fridge, pump) draws 3–5 times its running power for a few seconds while starting. The inverter's surge rating (written on its datasheet, usually 1.5 to 2 times the rated VA for a few seconds) must cover this.

Other things to check on the inverterDC input voltage = system battery voltage (Step 4) · AC output 230 V, 50 Hz · Pure sine wave output for fans and computers · Built-in charger only if a grid backup is also needed.

    Choose the system (battery) DC voltage

    The DC voltage used for sizing the inverter, battery and PV array is called the System DC Voltage. Bigger systems use higher voltage.

    Recommended system voltage

    Less than 1 kW12 V
    1 – 3 kW24 V
    3 – 8 kW48 – 96 V
    10 – 20 kW120 – 240 V

    This trainer picks: 3–5 kW → 48 V, 5–10 kW → 96 V, 10–15 kW → 120 V, 15–20 kW → 240 V. The table has no row for 8–10 kW, so 96 V is used. Above 20 kW, a qualified engineer designs the system.

    Why higher voltage for bigger systems?

    Current (A) = Power (W) ÷ Voltage (V)

    For the same power, if the voltage is doubled the current becomes half. Less current means thinner cables, smaller fuses and MCBs, less heating, and less voltage drop. That is the only reason we go to higher voltage.

    But a higher voltage needs more batteries in series (24 V = 2 batteries of 12 V). For a small system that is a waste of money. So small systems stay at 12 V.

      Battery bank size (Ah) and connection

      The battery must store enough energy for the chosen number of days without sun, without discharging too deeply.

      Two new words

      Autonomy: the number of days a fully charged battery can run the loads with no charging from the panel (cloudy days). Homes usually use 2 days; hospitals and telecom towers use 3–5 days.

      Depth of Discharge (DoD): how much of the battery we allow to be used. A lead-acid battery lives longer if we use only part of it. With DoD = 60 %, we use 60 % and always keep 40 % inside.

      Battery Ah = (Inverter input Wh × Autonomy days) ÷ (System V × DoD)

      This formula comes from Wh = V × Ah. We divide the energy by the voltage to get ampere-hours.

      Series or parallel?

      Series (+ of one to − of next) adds voltage: decided by System V ÷ Battery V.
      Parallel (+ to +, − to −) adds Ah: decided by Required Ah ÷ Ah of one battery.

      Field practiceUse solar tubular batteries with a C10 Ah rating. Try to keep 4 or fewer parallel strings, because current does not share equally in many parallel strings. If more are needed, choose a bigger Ah battery or 2 V cells. Use batteries of the same make, Ah and age.

        PV array size (watts peak)

        The panels must put back into the battery, in one day of sunshine, all the energy the battery gave out plus the battery's own loss.

        Peak sun hours (PSH)

        Sunlight is weak in the morning, strongest at noon, and weak again in the evening. We imagine the whole day's sunlight squeezed into a few hours of full sun at 1000 W/m². That number of hours is called peak sun hours.

        In Kerala the yearly average is about 4.5 to 5.5 kWh/m²/day. In the monsoon months it can fall to about 3.5–4. Use the value for your critical design month.

        Battery input energy = Battery output Wh ÷ Battery efficiency
        PV array watts = Battery input energy ÷ Peak sun hours
        Why battery efficiency?A battery never gives back all the energy put into it. If it gives back 95 %, the panel must put in 2690 ÷ 0.95 = 2832 Wh to get 2690 Wh out.
        Field practiceThe book example uses 95 % battery efficiency and no other loss. Real lead-acid batteries return about 80–85 % of the energy. Panels also give less than their rating because of heat, dust and cable loss. Field designers therefore use a derating factor of about 0.75–0.85. Try the "Field practice" option and see how the array grows.

          Choose standard panels and arrange the array

          Panels are sold only in certain ratings. We choose a panel whose voltage suits the battery, then find how many in series and in parallel.

          Panel voltage must suit the battery

          A 12 V lead-acid battery needs about 14.4 V to charge fully. A 36-cell panel gives about 17–18 V at maximum power (Vmp). That is a little above 14.4 V, so it charges a 12 V battery well even when hot. This is why a 36-cell panel is called a "12 V panel".

          Standard panel ratings in the market (typical values)

          Panel typeWpVmpVocIscSuits battery (PWM)
          Panels in series = System V ÷ Panel nominal V (for PWM-matched panels)
          Parallel strings = PV watts required ÷ (Wp of one × panels in series) → round UP
          What does "No PWM match – use MPPT" mean?A PWM controller is only a switch: it pulls the panel down to the battery voltage, and the panel then gives about its normal current (Imp). For a 540 Wp panel (Vmp 41.6 V, Imp 12.98 A):
          BatteryCharging voltagePower with PWM
          12 V14.4 V12.98 × 14.4 ≈ 187 W (≈65 % wasted)
          24 V28.8 V12.98 × 28.8 ≈ 374 W (≈31 % wasted)
          48 V57.6 VVmp 41.6 V is below 57.6 V — cannot fully charge
          An MPPT controller keeps the panel at 41.6 V and converts the full power down to the battery voltage at a higher current, so almost all of the 540 W reaches the battery.
          Big 540 Wp panels are not "12 V panels"Their Vmp is about 41 V. Connected to a 12 V battery through a simple PWM controller, most of that power is wasted. They need an MPPT controller (Step 9).

            Charge controller: voltage and current rating

            The controller sits between the panels and the battery. It must safely handle the highest voltage and the highest current the array can ever give.

            Why Voc and Isc, not Vmp and Imp?

            Voc (open-circuit voltage) is the highest voltage a panel gives, when no current flows, for example in the morning when the battery is disconnected. Isc (short-circuit current) is the highest current it gives. The controller must survive both, so we size it with these.

            Controller voltage = String Voc × Safety factor
            String Voc = Voc of one panel × Panels in series
            Controller current = Total Isc × Safety factor
            Total Isc = Isc of one panel × Parallel strings
            Why a safety factor (1.2)?On a cold, bright morning the panel voltage rises above Voc. On a very bright day with reflection from clouds, current rises above Isc. The 20 % extra covers these.
            RememberIn series: voltages add, current stays the same. In parallel: currents add, voltage stays the same.
