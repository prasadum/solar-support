Solar PV Module Technologies

Cell types, module formats, efficiency and price comparison, Indian standards and field applications (data as of mid-2026)
Prepared by PRASAD UM, VI, Vocational Instructor, NSTI Calicut

How a solar cell turns light into electricity

Every panel on a rooftop, a canal top or a satellite works on the same physical effect: the photovoltaic effect at a p–n junction. What differs between "mono", "poly", PERC, TOPCon or HJT is how cleverly the silicon is grown, cleaned up and contacted so that fewer of the freed electrons are lost before they reach the wires.

A crystalline silicon solar cell is a thin wafer, about 130–150 µm thick (thinner than two sheets of paper), made of two regions. One region is doped to have extra free electrons (n-type, typically phosphorus) and the other is doped to have "holes" or missing electrons (p-type, typically boron or gallium). Where these two regions meet, a built-in electric field forms across the depletion region. This field is the engine of the cell.

When a photon from sunlight with energy greater than the silicon band gap (1.12 eV, corresponding to wavelengths shorter than about 1,100 nm) is absorbed, it lifts an electron out of its bond, leaving a hole behind. If this electron–hole pair is generated near the junction, the built-in field separates them: electrons are swept to the n-side and holes to the p-side. Metal contacts on the front and rear collect these carriers, and if an external load is connected, a direct current flows.

A single cell gives an open-circuit voltage of only about 0.68–0.75 V, depending on the technology. To get useful voltage, cells are connected in series inside a module (60, 66, 72, 120, 132 or 144 cells/half-cells), modules are connected in series to form a string, and strings in parallel form an array.

From cell to array

Three generations of PV technology

Engineers usually group solar technologies into generations. The first generation is wafer-based crystalline silicon: monocrystalline and polycrystalline. It accounts for roughly 97% of all modules shipped worldwide today. Within mono silicon, the cell architecture has evolved rapidly: Al-BSF (till about 2017), then PERC (2017–2023), and now TOPCon, HJT and back-contact (BC) cells. The second generation is thin film: cadmium telluride (CdTe), copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS) and amorphous silicon (a-Si), deposited as micrometre-thin layers on glass or flexible substrates. The third generation includes emerging technologies such as perovskite, perovskite-on-silicon tandem, organic PV and multi-junction III-V cells used in space.

Common misconception. "Monocrystalline" and "TOPCon" are not competing choices. Mono describes how the silicon crystal is grown; TOPCon describes how the cell is built on that wafer. Practically every TOPCon, HJT and back-contact module sold today uses a monocrystalline n-type wafer. When a dealer says "mono panel", ask which cell technology it is: PERC or TOPCon makes a measurable difference to yield.

Monocrystalline silicon (mono c-Si)

A monocrystalline wafer is cut from a single, continuous crystal of silicon with no grain boundaries. Electrons move through it with less resistance and less recombination, which is why mono cells are the most efficient silicon cells and now dominate the market.

How it is made: the Czochralski (CZ) process

High-purity polysilicon chunks (9N–11N purity, i.e. 99.9999999% or better) are melted in a quartz crucible at about 1,420 °C. A small seed crystal with the required crystal orientation is dipped into the melt and slowly pulled upward while rotating. Silicon atoms freeze onto the seed in the same orientation, forming a cylindrical ingot up to about 300 mm in diameter and several metres long. A dopant is added to the melt: boron or gallium for p-type, phosphorus for n-type. Gallium replaced boron in p-type wafers around 2020 because boron-oxygen pairs cause light-induced degradation (LID).

The round ingot is squared into a block with rounded corners, and diamond-wire saws slice it into wafers. Modern formats are M10 (182 × 182 mm), G12 (210 × 210 mm), and the rectangular 182 × 210 mm (often called 210R or G12R) format that now dominates new 2026 modules because it fits standard container and roof dimensions well.

How to identify it

Mono cells look uniformly dark blue-black. Older cells had a distinct "pseudo-square" shape with cut corners, leaving small white diamonds between cells; today's wafers are almost fully square, so modules look almost uniformly black. The surface is textured with microscopic random pyramids formed by alkaline etching, which trap light and reduce reflection.

p-type mono versus n-type mono

Until about 2023, most mono cells were p-type (boron- or gallium-doped base) and used the PERC architecture. Since then, the industry has switched almost entirely to n-type mono wafers (phosphorus-doped base) for TOPCon, HJT and back-contact cells. n-type silicon is more tolerant of metallic impurities such as iron, has no boron–oxygen LID, and has longer minority-carrier lifetime, allowing higher voltage and higher efficiency. The trade-off is a slightly more complex and costly cell process, which has now largely been absorbed by scale.

Advantages

  • Highest efficiency among silicon technologies, so fewer panels and less roof area per kW.
  • Better low-light and high-temperature performance than poly.
  • Base wafer for all modern high-efficiency cells (TOPCon, HJT, BC).
  • Uniform black appearance is preferred for homes and buildings.

Limitations

  • Crystal growth is energy-intensive; higher embodied energy than thin film.
  • Rigid glass module; weight about 25–33 kg for large formats.
  • Performance still drops 0.25–0.35% for every °C rise in cell temperature.
Field practice. For a typical Kerala rooftop under PM Surya Ghar, the module offered will almost always be a mono-crystalline n-type TOPCon (or occasionally mono PERC) module in the 540–620 Wp class. For subsidy eligibility, the module must be on MNRE's ALMM List-I and, from 1 June 2026, must contain DCR cells from ALMM List-II. Check the datasheet and the DCR certificate, not just the word "mono".

Polycrystalline silicon (multi c-Si)

Polycrystalline (also called multicrystalline) silicon is cast as a large block containing many small crystals. It was cheaper to make and dominated the market until around 2018, but it has now been almost completely replaced by mono silicon.

How it is made: directional solidification (casting)

Instead of slowly pulling a single crystal, molten silicon is poured into, or melted inside, a large square quartz crucible and allowed to cool from the bottom upward in a controlled way. The silicon freezes into many crystal grains of different orientations, each a few millimetres to centimetres across. The block (about 800–1,000 kg) is cut into square bricks and sliced into square wafers. Because the crucible is square, there is no corner waste, and the process uses less energy and time than CZ pulling.

Why the sparkly, blue look?

Each grain has a different crystal orientation, so it reflects light differently. This gives the characteristic speckled "metal flake" blue appearance. Acidic texturing, used on poly, cannot form the uniform pyramids that alkaline texturing produces on mono, so poly cells reflect more light and look lighter blue.

Why efficiency is lower

Grain boundaries are crystal defects. Electrons and holes recombine at these boundaries and at impurities that collect there, before they can reach the contacts. Poly wafers also carry more dislocations and metal impurities from the crucible. As a result, poly cells reach about 18–20% (PERC poly up to about 21%) while mono PERC easily reached 23%. Once the cost of mono wafers fell after 2016–2018 due to faster CZ pullers and diamond-wire sawing, the higher yield of mono outweighed the price saving of poly.

Where it still makes sense

  • Replacement of a failed module in an existing poly string (matching voltage and current).
  • Very small off-grid uses where any available low-cost panel will do.
  • Training labs: the visible grain structure makes it ideal for explaining crystal defects.

Why it is no longer recommended

  • Needs about 20–30% more roof area than TOPCon for the same kW.
  • Poorer temperature coefficient hurts badly in hot Indian summers.
  • Fewer manufacturers, so warranty support for new purchases is uncertain.
  • Not competitive for new ALMM/DCR subsidy projects.
Replacement tip. If one poly module in a 2016-era string fails, do not insert a modern 580 W TOPCon module into the same string. Its Imp (around 13–14 A) is far higher than the old module's Imp (about 8–9 A) and its voltage is different, so the mismatch can cause hot spots or wasted capacity. Use a module with closely matching Imp, or re-design the string.

Thin-film modules: CdTe, CIGS and amorphous silicon

Thin-film cells deposit a light-absorbing layer only 1–4 µm thick, about a hundred times thinner than a silicon wafer, directly on glass, metal foil or plastic. Cells are formed by laser scribing the coated sheet into long narrow strips connected in series.

Cadmium telluride (CdTe)

CdTe is the only thin-film technology with large-scale commercial success, led by First Solar, which also operates a large manufacturing plant in Tamil Nadu. Modules reach about 18–19.5% efficiency. CdTe has a band gap of about 1.45 eV, close to ideal for sunlight, and a lower temperature coefficient (about −0.28 to −0.32%/°C) than PERC, so it performs well in hot, dusty desert plants. It also responds better to diffuse and humid-atmosphere light. Its weaknesses are lower efficiency than TOPCon and the use of cadmium, which demands a take-back and recycling programme.

CIGS (copper indium gallium selenide)

CIGS reaches about 14–17% in modules and can be made on flexible substrates. It is used in some building-integrated (BIPV) façades, curved surfaces and lightweight roofs, but manufacturing has struggled to compete on cost with silicon.

Amorphous silicon (a-Si)

a-Si has no long-range crystal order. It is cheap to deposit but only 6–9% efficient and suffers Staebler–Wronski light-induced degradation in the first months. Today it is found mostly in calculators, watches, garden lights and indoor devices, where its good response to fluorescent and LED light matters more than efficiency.

Misconception. Thin film is not "weaker" in every situation. Per kWp installed, a CdTe plant in a very hot site can produce comparable or slightly higher energy than a PERC plant because of its lower temperature loss. What it needs is more land and more mounting structure per kW, because each square metre produces less power.

Cell architectures: PERC, TOPCon, HJT and back contact

All four are built on monocrystalline wafers. They differ in how they passivate the surfaces (stop electrons from recombining) and how they contact the silicon. Each step forward cut a specific loss, and module efficiency rose from about 17% (Al-BSF) to about 24% (BC).

Evolution in one line

Al-BSF (fully covered aluminium rear, about 19–20% cell) → PERC (rear passivated with Al₂O₃/SiNₓ, local contacts, about 23%) → TOPCon (n-type, passivating tunnel oxide + doped poly-Si contact on the rear, about 25–26.5%) → HJT (amorphous silicon layers on both sides, about 25.5–26.5%) → BC (all contacts on the rear, no front shading, about 26–27%) → perovskite–silicon tandem (two absorbers stacked, record cells above 34% in the lab).

Why passivation matters. The surface of a wafer is full of "dangling bonds" where electrons are trapped and lost. In PERC only part of the surface is passivated and the metal contacts still touch bare silicon, which is a big loss. TOPCon and HJT put an extra thin layer between the metal and the silicon, so carriers pass through but recombination at the contact is greatly reduced. That is why their open-circuit voltage is about 30–50 mV higher than PERC, which directly lifts efficiency.

Module formats: half-cut, bifacial, glass–glass, busbars and wafer size

Cell technology decides how efficiently each cell converts light. Module design decides how much of that power reaches the cable. These features are combined freely: a typical 2026 module is "n-type TOPCon, half-cut, 16BB/0BB, bifacial, glass–glass, 182×210 mm".

Half-cut cells

A full cell is laser-cut into two halves. Each half produces the same voltage but only half the current. Because resistive loss in the ribbons and fingers is P = I²R, halving the current reduces the loss in each conductor to one quarter. The module is wired as two identical halves (upper and lower) connected in parallel, with the junction box placed in the middle of the module.

Benefits: around 2–3% more output from the same cells, lower hot-spot temperature, and better shade tolerance. If the lower row is shaded by a parapet wall, only the lower half loses power while the upper half keeps producing. A cut cell is also mechanically less stressed, reducing micro-crack spread. Most modules now use half-cut cells; some premium designs use one-third cuts or shingled strips.

Bifacial modules

A bifacial module collects light on both faces. The cell has a grid (not a full metal sheet) on the rear, and the module uses a transparent rear glass or transparent backsheet. Light reflected from the ground or roof reaches the rear and adds 5–25% extra energy, depending on the surface reflectance (albedo), mounting height and row spacing.

The bifaciality factor is rear efficiency ÷ front efficiency: PERC about 70%, TOPCon about 80–85%, HJT about 85–95%. Bifacial gain is largest over white roofs or white gravel (albedo 0.5–0.8), on elevated structures, and on single-axis trackers. On a flush-mounted dark sheet roof, the rear sees almost no light, so a bifacial module gives little extra benefit.

Common error during installation. Large-format modules (about 2.4 × 1.1 m) are sensitive to clamping position. Clamping outside the manufacturer's permitted zones, or walking on the module, causes micro-cracks that show up later as snail trails and power loss. Always check the mechanical load rating (for example 5,400 Pa front / 2,400 Pa rear) and the clamping drawing in the installation manual.

How to read the comparison

Efficiency decides how much power you can fit on a given roof. For a 25 m² terrace, poly at 17% gives about 4.2 kWp while TOPCon at 23% gives about 5.7 kWp. Temperature coefficient decides how much is lost on hot days: at a cell temperature of 65 °C, a PERC module (−0.35%/°C) loses about 14% while an HJT module (−0.25%/°C) loses about 10%. Degradation decides what is left after 25–30 years: n-type modules typically guarantee 87–89% at 30 years, against about 80–83% at 25 years for older p-type modules.

Price per watt is only part of the economics. The correct comparison is the cost per kWh generated over the life of the system (LCOE), which includes yield, degradation and the balance-of-system cost per kW. A slightly more expensive, higher-efficiency module often wins when roof space, structure cost or land is the limiting factor.

Reading a module datasheet

STC (Standard Test Conditions): irradiance 1,000 W/m², cell temperature 25 °C, spectrum AM 1.5G. The nameplate power (Pmax or Wp) is measured at STC. Real rooftops rarely see a 25 °C cell; in Indian summer, cells commonly run at 55–70 °C.

NMOT / NOCT: nominal module (or cell) operating temperature at 800 W/m², 20 °C ambient and 1 m/s wind. Typical values are 41–45 °C. It is used to estimate real cell temperature.

Voc, Isc, Vmp, Imp: open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, and voltage and current at the maximum power point. Voc at the coldest expected temperature decides how many modules can be placed in series without exceeding the inverter's maximum DC voltage. Isc decides cable size and inverter MPPT current rating.

Temperature coefficients: for Pmax (about −0.25 to −0.40%/°C), Voc (about −0.24 to −0.30%/°C) and Isc (about +0.04 to +0.05%/°C).

Power tolerance: usually 0 to +5 W, meaning the module will never test below nameplate.

Warranty: product warranty 12–25 years; linear performance warranty such as "1% first-year loss, 0.4% per year thereafter, ≥ 87.4% at 30 years" for n-type modules.

Kerala-specific point. Near the coast (within roughly 1–5 km of the sea), specify modules certified to IEC 61701 at a high severity level, glass–glass construction where possible, and stainless or hot-dip galvanised structures with proper isolation between dissimilar metals. Salt corrosion of frames, junction boxes and MC4 connectors is a leading cause of early failures in coastal districts.

Real-world applications

The best technology depends on the site: available area, temperature, shading, the surface below the modules, weight limits and whether a subsidy requires DCR content.

Examples from Kerala and India

Residential rooftops under PM Surya Ghar (KSEB net metering)

A typical 3 kW home system in Kerala uses five or six 540–620 Wp DCR mono modules (PERC or, increasingly, TOPCon), a 3 kW on-grid inverter and a bidirectional meter installed by KSEB. Half-cut modules are preferred because terraces often have water tanks, parapets and coconut trees causing partial shade. On hot concrete terraces, the better temperature coefficient of TOPCon adds noticeable summer generation.

Cochin International Airport (CIAL)

In 2015 CIAL became widely known as the world's first airport fully powered by solar energy. Its ground-mounted and canopy plants have since grown to several tens of MWp, including carport-style installations over parking areas and solar plants on airport land combined with vegetable farming beneath the panels (an early Indian example of agrivoltaics).

Floating solar

KSEB's floating plant on the Banasura Sagar reservoir in Wayanad was among India's earliest floating installations. Nationally, NTPC's 100 MW floating plant at Ramagundam (Telangana) is one of the largest. Water cools the modules, improving yield, and reduces evaporation. Glass–glass modules with high PID and corrosion resistance are preferred because of constant humidity.

Solar ferry "Aditya"

Kerala State Water Transport Department's Aditya, operating between Vaikom and Thavanakkadavu since 2017, is India's first solar-powered ferry. Roof-mounted mono modules charge the battery bank, showing how PV is used in transport where weight and space are limited.

Utility-scale solar parks

Bhadla (Rajasthan, about 2.2 GW) and Pavagada (Karnataka, about 2 GW) are among the largest solar parks in the world. New phases use bifacial n-type TOPCon modules on single-axis trackers. Desert sand and light soil give useful rear-side gain, and robotic waterless cleaning is used to handle dust.

Agriculture: PM-KUSUM solar pumps and agrivoltaics

Under PM-KUSUM, farmers install standalone solar pumps (Component B) or solarise grid-connected pumps (Component C). Modules are mono PERC or TOPCon with a variable-frequency pump controller. In agrivoltaics, modules are raised 2.5–4 m above crops; bifacial and semi-transparent glass–glass modules let diffuse light reach plants.

Commercial and industrial rooftops

Factories, hospitals and institutions (including ITIs and NSTIs) install 50 kW–2 MW on metal sheet roofs. Lightweight framed TOPCon modules on rail-less clamps are common; bifacial gain is small on dark sheet roofs, so monofacial modules are often chosen.

Thin film and specialised uses

CdTe modules are used in large hot-climate plants; CIGS and perovskite prototypes appear in BIPV façades. a-Si cells power calculators and indoor sensors. Spacecraft, including ISRO satellites, use high-efficiency III–V multi-junction cells (about 30% in space) because mass and area matter more than cost.

Off-grid and small systems

Solar street lights, traffic blinkers, telecom towers, fencing energisers and remote homes use small 20–400 W mono modules with batteries. Here robustness and battery quality matter more than the last percent of efficiency.

Selection rule of thumb (2026). Limited roof and subsidy project: DCR mono TOPCon, half-cut. Shaded or complex roof: half-cut or shingled modules, plus module-level optimisers or microinverters. White roof, elevated structure or tracker: bifacial glass–glass TOPCon or HJT. Very hot site with plenty of land: TOPCon, HJT or CdTe. Coastal site: glass–glass, IEC 61701-certified. Premium residential look: all-black BC or TOPCon.

Worked numericals and calculators

Each calculator is preloaded with the values from the first worked example. Change any input to explore.

1. Module efficiency

Efficiency η = Pmax ÷ (G × A) × 100, where G = 1,000 W/m² at STC and A = module area in m².

Worked example 1.1 – A TOPCon module rated 580 Wp measures 2,278 × 1,134 mm. Find its efficiency.
A = 2.278 m × 1.134 m = 2.583 m² Pin = 1,000 W/m² × 2.583 m² = 2,583 W η = 580 ÷ 2,583 × 100 = 22.45 %
Worked example 1.2 – An older poly module rated 330 Wp measures 1,956 × 992 mm. Find its efficiency and the area needed for 3 kWp.
A = 1.956 × 0.992 = 1.940 m² η = 330 ÷ (1,000 × 1.940) × 100 = 17.0 % Modules for 3 kWp = 3,000 ÷ 330 = 9.09 → 10 modules → 19.4 m² (The TOPCon module needs 6 × 2.583 = 15.5 m² for 3.48 kWp.)

2. Power at real operating temperature

Cell temperature Tcell = Tamb + (NOCT − 20) ÷ 800 × G. Output P = PSTC × (G ÷ 1000) × [1 + γ × (Tcell − 25)], where γ is the Pmax temperature coefficient (negative).

Worked example 2.1 – A 580 Wp TOPCon module (γ = −0.29 %/°C, NOCT 45 °C) on a Kochi terrace at noon: ambient 34 °C, G = 1,000 W/m².
Tcell = 34 + (45 − 20) ÷ 800 × 1,000 = 34 + 31.25 = 65.25 °C ΔT = 65.25 − 25 = 40.25 °C Loss = 0.29 × 40.25 = 11.67 % P = 580 × (1 − 0.1167) = 512.3 W
Worked example 2.2 – Same conditions, but a 580 Wp PERC module with γ = −0.35 %/°C.
Loss = 0.35 × 40.25 = 14.09 % P = 580 × (1 − 0.1409) = 498.3 W TOPCon advantage = 512.3 − 498.3 = 14.0 W per module (about 2.8 %)

3. Rooftop system sizing and annual energy

Required kWp = monthly units ÷ (30 × specific yield). Kerala rooftops typically yield about 3.8–4.2 kWh per kWp per day (roughly 1,400–1,500 kWh/kWp/year).

Worked example 3.1 – A house uses 360 units per month. Size a system with 580 Wp modules (2.583 m² each) at 4.0 kWh/kWp/day.
kWp needed = 360 ÷ (30 × 4.0) = 3.0 kWp Modules = 3,000 ÷ 580 = 5.17 → 6 modules Actual kWp = 6 × 0.580 = 3.48 kWp Annual kWh = 3.48 × 4.0 × 365 = 5,081 kWh Module area = 6 × 2.583 = 15.5 m² (allow ≈ 20% extra for access/spacing → ≈ 18.6 m²)
Worked example 3.2 – Module cost for the 3.48 kWp system at DCR price ₹25/Wp versus non-DCR ₹15/Wp.
DCR = 3,480 Wp × ₹25 = ₹87,000 Non-DCR = 3,480 Wp × ₹15 = ₹52,200 (Only DCR modules qualify for PM Surya Ghar subsidy.)

4. Half-cut resistive loss and bifacial gain

Worked example 4.1 – A cell ribbon path has resistance 0.010 Ω. Full-cell current 13.6 A; half-cut current 6.8 A.
Full cell : P = I²R = 13.6² × 0.010 = 1.85 W Half cell : P = 6.8² × 0.010 = 0.46 W (one quarter)
Worked example 4.2 – A 580 Wp bifacial TOPCon module (bifaciality 80%) on a white-painted roof where rear irradiance is 12% of front irradiance.
Bifacial gain = 0.80 × 12 % = 9.6 % Equivalent power = 580 × 1.096 = 635.7 W
